The Net Loft Gallery reopened Saturday after a two-year closure for structural repairs, launching with “Working Hands,” a photography and mixed-media exhibition built around contemporary waterfront labor.

The building is a converted net loft on Pier Six, put up in 1911 and used for drying and mending seine nets until 1974. It became a gallery in 1996. In 2024 an engineer's survey found rot in eleven of the main floor joists and a failing east-wall sill plate, and the board closed the space with four days' notice, mid-exhibition, while a show of watercolors was still on the walls.

Curator Elias Morn spent much of that closure deciding what the reopening should not be. What it is not is sepia. “Working Hands” contains no fishing boats at golden hour and no black-and-white portraits of men who died in 1960. It shows welders at the dry dock, line cooks working the 5:40 ferry galley, a crane signaller, and two net menders photographed under flat LED shop lights that make everything look exactly as bright and unromantic as it is.

“Nostalgia is a kind of forgetting,” Morn said at the preview. “If I photograph this harbor as though the interesting part ended fifty years ago, I am telling four thousand people who work here right now that they are a footnote to their own jobs. I would rather the pictures be less beautiful and more true.”

> The building itself is part of the show.

Exposed beams carry small brass plates noting which sections were replaced and which were saved. Fourteen plates read REPLACED 2025; nine read ORIGINAL 1911. Visitors can walk a catwalk once used for hanging gear, now railed to code, that runs the length of the east wall about four meters above the floor and gives an unnerving view straight down onto the largest prints.

## What the repairs cost, and who paid

The capital campaign raised $2.4 million against a final construction cost of $2.61 million. The gap was closed with a bridge loan the board is still carrying, at a payment of roughly $4,900 a month through 2031.

Board chair Rosalind Teague-Ihara is the person most willing to say plainly that the reopening does not resolve the finances. “We have a beautiful building and a loan,” she said. “A corporate underwriter covers lights and insurance. Memberships cover programming risk, which is the polite way of saying memberships are what let us mount a show that might not sell. Neither of those covers a roof. We have not had the endowment conversation and we are going to have it before the next capital surprise, not after, because the last time we had it after, we closed for two years.”

Accessibility upgrades made during the closure — a ramped entry replacing the four-step loading threshold, hearing loop coverage in both rooms, and designated quiet hours on Wednesday mornings — are now part of the pitch rather than a line in a grant report. Three visitors at the preview told staff, unprompted, that they had not been inside the building since before 2019 because they could not get in.

Not everyone in the neighborhood is celebrating. Aurelio Banch, who runs a gear repair shop two doors down and whose rent has risen twice since the campaign was announced, said he had been photographed for the show and had mixed feelings about the result.

“The picture is good. Elias is good,” Banch said. “But I have watched this before. A gallery reopens, the pier gets pretty, and in six years the people in the pictures cannot afford to be on the pier. I am not saying don't do it. I am saying somebody should be counting the leases as carefully as they counted the joists.”

Teague-Ihara said the board has no answer to that beyond the gallery's own lease, which it holds through 2044 at a fixed rate from the harbor authority.

## Opening weekend

Attendance beat staff projections, helped by clear weather and a free first hour for students: 1,880 across Saturday and Sunday against a forecast of 1,200. Gift shop sales skewed toward the exhibition catalog rather than novelty items, a metric curators quietly cheer.

Opening night drew a mix of artists and longshore families in roughly equal number, which staff attributed to Morn having personally invited every subject and told each to bring whoever they wanted. Several of the portrait subjects arrived, read their own wall labels, and corrected them in pencil where details were wrong — a crane model, a local name for a knot, the year someone started on the docks. Staff have left every correction visible. One label now has three crossings-out and a phone number in the margin that nobody has been able to explain.

Admission is free on the first Sunday of each month. A companion oral-history booth, a soundproofed box near the loading doors, will record visitors through autumn; the recordings go to the basin's public archive.

Artists in the show have begun side conversations about a touring version scaled for smaller halls up the coast. Logistics, not ambition, will decide it — several works are mounted on salvaged loft timber and weigh more than any of the candidate venues can hang.

On Saturday evening the crew rolled the loading doors open, because it was warm, and the sound of a tug working the far side of the basin came in over the prints of the men who work on it. Nobody closed the doors. The harbor kept talking through the room until nine.
