Port Meridian schools will double the share of locally sourced produce in lunch contracts under a new RFP that scores bidders on regional purchasing, not only unit price.

The rewrite is narrower than the phrase "farm to school" usually implies. Under the current agreement, roughly 11 percent of the district's produce spend stays within a hundred miles of the harbor. The new scoring rubric, approved by the school board on a 6-1 vote in March, sets a floor of 22 percent by the 2027-28 school year, with an interim checkpoint of 16 percent next spring. Price still carries 45 points of the 100-point evaluation. Regional sourcing carries 20. Delivery reliability, food-safety documentation, and substitution flexibility split the rest.

Nutrition director Carla Mendez said the goal is resilient supply and better flavor, not Instagram trays. "Kids notice when carrots taste like something," she said.

Mendez has run the district's kitchens for nine years, long enough to be unsentimental about it. Her office on the second floor of the Bayard Avenue annex has a whiteboard tracking three numbers: meals served daily (18,400 across 31 sites), average plate cost ($1.62 for the food component), and the running total of federal reimbursement. None of those numbers move much. The produce line does, and that is the lever she chose.

> Small farms worried about volume consistency.

The concern was not theoretical. At a bidders' conference in February held in the cafeteria of Rope Walk Elementary, growers from the river valley listed the things that end a contract: a hailstorm in June, a broker who pays faster, a single week when a farm cannot fill an order for 900 pounds of romaine. Delia Sarkissian, who farms 40 acres of brassicas and root crops outside Marrow Bend, put it plainly. "A school district is a wonderful customer right up until the Tuesday you cannot deliver," she said. "Then you are a liability with a truck."

The district answered with flexible substitution lists and a processing partner that can cut and bag to kitchen specs. The substitution lists group produce by function rather than by item - a "crisp raw" category, a "roast" category, a "braise" category - so a shortfall in one crop can be covered by another without a contract amendment. The processing agreement, with Harbor Fresh Cut on the Fourth Basin, gives small growers a place to send whole product they cannot wash, cut, and pack to school specification themselves. Harbor Fresh Cut charges 14 cents a pound for the service; the district absorbs it inside the produce line rather than passing it to growers.

## The volume problem cuts both ways

Not everyone in the district's supply chain considers the change an improvement. Ronan Whitby, regional sales manager for Coastwise Provisions, which has held the district's primary produce contract since 2019, argued at the February conference that the rubric confuses geography with resilience.

"We source from four states," Whitby said. "When one region gets a wet spring, we shift. A twenty-two percent local floor means twenty-two percent of your produce is exposed to one weather system. That is not diversification. That is the opposite." He also noted that Coastwise already buys from six of the same valley farms the district now wants to reach directly, and that the rubric gives no credit for that.

Mendez does not dismiss the argument. She says the substitution lists exist precisely because she expects a bad season within the first three years, and that the district built a 4 percent contingency into the produce budget - about $71,000 annually - to buy conventional wholesale when a local order collapses. "If the answer to one hailstorm is that we abandon the whole approach, we designed it wrong," she said.

Parents at a forum asked about allergens and cultural menus. Mendez said both remain constraints - and opportunities - as recipes rotate. The district's menu committee, which includes four parent representatives and two cafeteria managers, has been testing a rotation that leans on cabbage, winter squash, and dried beans through February, when the valley has little else. One parent, a nurse named Priya Anand who has a child with a tree-nut allergy at Steepbank Middle, said the substitution lists worried her more than the sourcing did. "Flexibility for the kitchen is uncertainty for me," she said. "I need the label on Thursday to match the label on Monday." Mendez agreed to post the substitution log publicly each week, an unglamorous concession that may end up being the most consequential thing to come out of the forum.

Implementation begins with elementary sites, where waste audits will measure whether better produce actually gets eaten. Eleven schools will weigh tray scrapings twice a month for the first year, separating produce waste from everything else. The baseline, taken last October, was discouraging: 38 percent of served vegetables came back uneaten. Mendez has told the board she will consider the pilot a failure if that number does not fall below 30 percent by the second audit cycle, regardless of how good the sourcing story reads.

Bids close on May 22. Award is scheduled for the June 9 board meeting, which gives the winning vendors roughly ten weeks to secure grower commitments before the first delivery of the fall term.

At Rope Walk, cafeteria manager Odell Pryce has already made one change he did not need a contract for. He moved the salad bar out from the wall so students pass it on both sides of the line rather than doubling back. Uptake went from a quarter of students to just under half in three weeks. "You can buy the best beet in the county," he said, wiping the rail. "If it is behind a kid's shoulder, it goes in the bin."
