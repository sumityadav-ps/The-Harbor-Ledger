Travel operators on the outer islands report that shoulder season — late spring and mid-autumn — now outperforms peak weeks for visitor satisfaction scores, even as absolute numbers stay lower.

The gap is not small. The three-island visitor survey, administered by the ferry authority since 2019, put mean satisfaction at 4.6 of 5 for October arrivals against 3.9 for the first two weeks of August. Peak-week scores have fallen every year since 2022. Shoulder scores have risen in four of the last five.

Inns discount without looking desperate. Ferry lines run on time more often. Trails are walkable without a conga line. "People remember the light," said innkeeper Maris Cole of South Haven, who has run the eleven-room Fennwick House for twenty-two years and now fills more October nights than August ones.

Cole's rates tell the story plainly: $215 in peak, $158 from the last week of September, $142 after the tenth of October. She stopped calling it a discount in her marketing three years ago. "The minute you say discount, you have told the guest they are getting the leftovers," she said. "I say October rate. It is a rate. August is the one with the surcharge."

> The tradeoff is services.

Some restaurants close two weekdays. Bicycle rental fleets shrink — the South Haven operator drops from 60 bikes to 18 after the first of October. The pharmacy on Ketch Island reduces to three days. Travelers who need full amenities should call ahead rather than trust last summer's hours.

The ferry timetable is the thing most people get wrong. From October 12, the last northbound sailing from Ketch leaves at 4:40 p.m., not 7:15. Miss it and there is no inventory on the island to absorb you; the two inns that stay open are frequently full because they are the only two.

Local guides are packaging half-day walks that end before dark ferry slots, a practical design that reduces stranded visitors and emergency calls. Volunteer rescue on Ketch logged 31 callouts last year, 22 of them in shoulder months, and coordinator Sable Ruiz says nearly all of them share one cause.

"It is not injuries. It is people who budgeted August daylight in October," Ruiz said. "The headland loop takes three hours either way. In August that ends in gold light. On the twentieth of October it ends in the dark, in wind, with a phone at nine percent."

## The labor problem underneath

If the trend holds, shoulder inventory may need more year-round workers rather than purely seasonal hires — a labor puzzle destinations are only beginning to price honestly.

The math is unforgiving. A seasonal hire can be housed in shared summer accommodation and paid for fourteen weeks. A worker who covers May and October as well needs housing on an island where year-round rental stock is measured in dozens, and needs something closer to a salary.

Deshan Merrow, who manages the Ketch Island co-op grocery, put a number on it. Extending his staffing to a genuine eight-month season would cost about $86,000 a year in additional wages and $31,000 in the housing subsidy he would have to offer to attract anyone. Shoulder revenue at current volumes covers roughly two-thirds of that.

"Everybody talks about shoulder season like it is free money you forgot to pick up," Merrow said. "It is money that arrives eight months a year against costs that arrive twelve."

He is one of the few operators willing to say publicly that the trend might be worse for islanders than for visitors. His argument: peak season is brutal but compressed, and it lets a year-round population earn its living in a burst and then have its home back. A stretched season spreads the same total revenue across more months of being a service economy, with the shoulder-week guest paying less for more attention.

"Ask anybody who lives here whether they want fewer good weeks or more mediocre ones," he said. "You will get an argument, not a consensus. That is the honest answer and nobody puts it in a brochure."

Operators are training staff to sell shoulder season without apologizing for closed kitchens. The script emphasizes light, space, and walking, not a thinner version of peak week. A new joint marketing campaign among three islands emphasizes quiet rather than urgency, and early analytics show longer average stays among those who book shoulder packages — 3.4 nights against 2.1 in peak.

Transport remains the hinge. Missed connections turn a calm itinerary into a scramble, so packages increasingly include buffer hours and clear refund rules when weather intervenes. The ferry authority cancelled eleven sailings last October, nine of them for wind over the Fennwick narrows.

Review scores mention silence as often as scenery. That feedback is reshaping room assignments and quiet-hours enforcement at smaller inns; Cole moved her two street-facing rooms out of the shoulder inventory entirely rather than field one more complaint about the 6 a.m. delivery truck.

Weather remains honest. Pack layers. The channel does not care about your itinerary screenshots.

Practical dates for anyone booking: rates drop on the outer three from September 24. The last full-service ferry timetable runs through October 11. The Fennwick House closes for the year on November 3, and Cole spends the following morning doing the same thing she has done for twenty-two Novembers — walking the eleven rooms with a notebook, writing down what broke.

Last year the list ran to nine items. She said the shortest it has ever been was four, in 2011, and the longest was thirty-one, the November after the summer everybody now calls the good one.
