The Channel Rowing Championship returns to Port Meridian next month, restoring a course that threads past active piers for the first time since 2019.

The course runs 2,000 metres down the eastern channel, starting off the grain terminal and finishing at the net loft park, with the last 400 metres inside a bend where the current sets hard toward the pier faces. Crews who have rowed it describe the same thing: the water goes strange at the 1,600 mark, and the crews who win are usually the ones who stopped being surprised by it.

Race windows are coordinated with harbor traffic control. Freighters get priority; shells get clear lanes in precise intervals. “It looks romantic until you see the radio traffic,” said regatta director Paul Voss. “There is a channel pilot who has the authority to end my afternoon and he does not care about my heat sheet, nor should he.”

The mechanics are unglamorous and absolute. Harbor traffic control has committed to two protected blocks each day - 6:40 to 9:15 in the morning and 4:30 to 6:50 in the evening - built around the tide and around the four scheduled deep-draft movements. Everything else is negotiated hour by hour. Voss’s operations plan allows 90 seconds between starts and a hard 11-minute abort window: if a vessel enters the upper channel, the last shell must be clear or the heat is scrubbed and re-rowed.

> Twelve university programs and four club boats are entered in the championship eights.

Local favorite Harbor College enters as defending women’s champion, having won the 2025 running on a lake course upstate by 1.9 seconds. Whether that result travels is genuinely open. Head coach Marguerite Sable has had her crews on the channel three mornings a week since March, and she is careful not to oversell the advantage.

“Everyone thinks local knowledge means we know a secret line,” Sable said. “There is no secret line. What we know is what a passing wake feels like at rate 38 so nobody flinches. That is worth maybe a length. It is not worth a season.”

## Why it went away, and why it came back

The event left the working waterfront after 2019 for reasons that had nothing to do with rowing. Insurance underwriters, reviewing a near-miss between a launch and a tug in the 2018 regatta, priced the channel course at a premium the organizing committee could not carry. For six years the championship rowed a lake 90 minutes inland - safe, flat, and, as several coaches noted without much affection, boring.

The return cost $214,000 in additional marine safety provision: eight escort launches instead of three, two dedicated radio operators, a chase medic on the water, and a standing agreement with the port authority that Voss says took fourteen months to draft and about four minutes to read.

Spectator barges will be limited to reduce wake. Only six commercial barges are permitted inside the course, moored and stationary, at a fee of $1,200 each. Private craft are excluded entirely from the lower channel during race blocks, a restriction that has not been universally popular.

Shore viewing at the net loft park is free, with a paid grandstand near the finish line funding youth learn-to-row scholarships. The grandstand seats 900 at $28 and has already sold 640. Last year’s scholarship fund put 31 students through a summer programme; the committee’s target this year is 45.

## The objection from the water

Not everyone on the channel wanted the regatta back, and the strongest complaint comes from people who work there.

Eberhard Klas skippers a bunker barge that services vessels at the grain terminal. His run is unglamorous, scheduled tight, and does not adjust well. The protected blocks fall directly across two of his four daily windows.

“A regatta is a lovely thing and I have nothing against a young person in a boat,” Klas said, standing on a dock that smelled of diesel and low tide. “But I move fuel on a clock somebody else set, and when the channel closes for a heat, my customer does not extend my deadline. Three days a year I will be late, I will eat the fee, and nobody who sold a grandstand ticket will hear about it.”

Voss does not dispute the arithmetic. The organizing committee has set aside a $30,000 disruption fund for exactly this and has paid out of it twice in trial runs, though Klas notes drily that a claim form is not the same thing as a schedule.

Weather remains the wildcard. A strong southerly can stack short chop against the current and force delays. Organizers built a one-day reserve into the schedule and have a written protocol for shortening the course to 1,500 metres if conditions demand, a decision Voss must make by 5:30 in the morning.

Crews arrive from the eleventh. Boat trailers stage in the old cannery lot. Sable has one instruction for her novices before their first channel outing, and it is not about the current: when a freighter passes, you will hear the wash before you see it, and the only wrong thing to do is look.
