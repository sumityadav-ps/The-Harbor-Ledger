The school board opened a boundary redraw process driven by uneven enrollment and the opening of a renovated middle school on the east side.

The imbalance is stark enough that nobody at Tuesday's meeting argued against acting. Harrowgate Middle is at 118 percent of design capacity, with four classes meeting in converted spaces including a room that was a band storage annex until 2023. Fairmont Middle, three miles west, sits at 74 percent. The renovated east-side building, Deshler, reopens in August with room for 640 students and currently has an attendance area containing about 410.

Staff presented three map options. None pleased every neighborhood. Online comment forms filled with concerns about walkability, sibling splits, and program access — 1,940 submissions in the first eleven days, which the district's community engagement office says is more than the last three boundary processes combined.

Map A moves the fewest students, 310, and leaves Harrowgate at 104 percent. Map B moves 680 and balances all three buildings within four points of capacity. Map C, which staff describe as the technically cleanest and which nobody expects to survive, moves 890 students and redraws the elementary feeder pattern along with it.

> Board members pledged that special programs would not be quietly concentrated by the new lines.

Advocates asked for that pledge in a formal equity memo, not a meeting assurance.

The concern is specific and has history. Harrowgate hosts the district's dual-language program and its only middle-school special education self-contained classrooms for students with significant support needs. Under Map B, the census blocks that supply about 60 percent of dual-language enrollment move to Deshler, while the program itself stays put — meaning families would either follow the program on transfer or lose it.

"A pledge in a meeting is worth exactly as long as this board's membership," said Odalys Ferrante of the Ridge Parents Coalition, who has been through two prior redraws. "Three of you are up in November. I am not questioning anyone's sincerity. I am saying sincerity is not a governance instrument. Put it in the resolution."

Board president Gideon Marchetti said staff would return with draft memo language by the April meeting, and stopped short of committing to bind the resolution to it.

## The counter-argument nobody wants to make loudly

The most substantive dissent came from a member who supports redrawing and thinks the process is aimed at the wrong number.

"We are solving for building utilization because building utilization is the thing we can measure and control," said board member Prosper Achebe. "The reason Harrowgate is full is that eleven hundred housing units went up in its attendance area in six years and the reason Fairmont is empty is that its area is aging in place. In four years the enrollment projection has been wrong by more than eight percent every single year, always low on the east side. We are about to move six hundred and eighty children to fix a distribution that our own model cannot predict twenty-four months out."

Achebe's proposal — a smaller Map A move now, paired with a commitment to revisit in three years — has not attracted a second, largely because a board that redraws every three years is a board that redraws forever. Ferrante, who disagrees with him, called it the only argument at the table that was about children rather than square footage.

Achebe's underlying complaint about the projection model is not disputed. The district's demographer, contracted since 2019, told the board in January that unit-level residential data from the county arrives eleven months in arrears and that the model's east-side error band is roughly plus or minus 9 percent at the three-year horizon.

A decision is expected by late spring to give families summer to adjust. The board has set April 14 for a revised map, May 12 for public hearing, and June 2 for a final vote.

Transportation routing will be modeled before the final vote, a lesson from the last redraw's bus chaos. In 2021 the board approved a map in June and discovered in August that 140 students had been assigned to a school reachable only by a route crossing the Bellweather drawbridge, which opens on demand for commercial traffic. Ninety-one late arrivals were logged in September alone.

This time routing runs first. Staff will publish estimated door-to-door times for every affected block group, including the walk-shed analysis that the last process skipped, and have committed to flagging any assignment producing a projected ride over 38 minutes.

Community meetings use translators and childcare — a logistical cost the district now treats as basic legitimacy. The line item is $34,000 across the process. It was cut from the 2021 process at $11,000 and the district's own after-action review named that cut as a primary cause of the trust collapse that followed.

The February 24 session at Deshler drew 230 people, ran three hours, and used four languages. The childcare room had nineteen children in it and a sign-in sheet that filled both sides.

Deshler's hallways still smell of sealant. The renovation left one thing untouched at the insistence of an alumni group: a stretch of the north stairwell where students carved initials into a wooden handrail between 1954 and the building's closure in 2011. There are hundreds of them, worn smooth in places.

At the end of the February meeting, after the folding chairs were stacked, a woman in her sixties walked the stairwell with a phone light, moving slowly, looking for something specific. She found it about two-thirds of the way up — three letters, close to the wall — and stood there with her hand over them until the custodian came to lock the floor.
