The historical society released self-guided architecture walks covering warehouses, civic buildings, and mid-century offices that rarely make postcard racks.

There are three loops. The short one runs 2.4 kilometres through the old bonded warehouse district and takes about an hour at a distracted pace. The middle loop adds the civic quarter and the 1961 water board building, a slab of board-formed concrete that most residents have described to a visitor at some point as “the ugly one.” The long loop, 6.8 kilometres, ends at a chain-link fence above the dry dock, which is not a monument to anything and is the stop people talk about most.

Booklets are free at the library and visitor center; audio tracks work offline for stretches with poor signal under bridges. The society printed 4,000 in the first run and had given out 2,600 by the second week of April, a rate that has already forced a reprint budget conversation the board had planned to have in the autumn.

> Local architects volunteered research hours.

Fourteen of them, logging what project coordinator Estella Barrow tallies at 610 hours across nine months. The work was mostly archival: pulling permit drawings, matching them to what was actually built, and settling arguments. Barrow says the arguments were the productive part.

Each stop includes a “what almost happened” note about demolished alternatives - the proposal that lost, the block that was scheduled for clearance and survived because a funding round collapsed. Stop 7 on the middle loop stands outside a modest brick post office and describes the eleven-storey administrative tower that was approved for the site in 1968, fully drawn, and never financed. The tower would have taken the entire block. The booklet reproduces the elevation drawing at postcard size, which is somehow more unsettling than a photograph would be.

## Correcting the record, gently

One stop corrects a long-repeated myth about a façade that was never maritime-themed, just poorly painted. The building is the former Kestrel Provisions warehouse on Ropewalk Street, whose wave-like banding has been explained to tourists for four decades as a shipping-line motif.

Barrow’s volunteers found the invoice. In 1954 the owners bought a discounted lot of two marine-grade paints that cured at different rates, applied them in horizontal bands to save money, and watched the seams bloom. The waves are a defect. The booklet says so in two sentences and includes the invoice number.

That entry has generated the only complaints the society has received. Two guides who have told the maritime version for years told Barrow, not unkindly, that she had taken something from them. One has kept telling it anyway, with a shrug and a footnote.

## The dry dock is the draw

Hotels report guests asking for the longer loop that ends at a working dry dock overlook. “People want industry, not only ice cream,” said concierge Luis Tran, who has started printing the long-loop map at his own front desk because he ran out of booklets in March.

The overlook is a strip of cracked asphalt with a view down into a dock where, on a good week, a coastal freighter sits dry with its hull exposed and men the size of thumbnails work along the keel blocks. There is no interpretive panel. There is a bench that somebody installed without permission in about 2011 and that the port authority has decided not to notice.

Local guides are packaging half-day walks that end before dark ferry slots, a practical design that reduces stranded visitors and emergency calls. Review scores for the participating inns mention silence as often as scenery, feedback that is reshaping room assignments and quiet-hours enforcement at the smaller properties.

Not everyone in the trade thinks the shoulder-season push is being priced honestly. Ondine Prahl, who owns a nine-room guesthouse on the north shore, argues that the walks are being used to sell a season the city cannot yet staff.

“I can fill rooms in October now. Good. With whom do I fill the kitchen?” she said. “My cook works May to September and drives a delivery van the rest of the year, and she is not giving that up for a maybe. Everyone wants year-round guests. Nobody wants to pay a year-round wage. Until somebody does, the shoulder season is just me working harder for the same money.”

Operators are training staff to sell shoulder season without apologizing for closed kitchens - the script emphasizes light, space, and walking rather than a thinner version of peak week. Prahl has read the script. She calls it well written and beside the point.

Transport remains the hinge. Missed connections turn a calm itinerary into a scramble, so packages increasingly include buffer hours and clear refund rules when weather intervenes. The ferry’s last winter departure is 4:15, which quietly determines how long any walk can be.

A second series on residential neighborhoods is planned if funding holds - the grant covering it runs out in November and has not been renewed. Barrow has already started the research anyway, in the evenings, working from a box of 1930s terrace plans that a retired surveyor left on the society’s doorstep in a bread crate, unlabeled, with a note that said only: someone should have these.
