Port Meridian’s planning department released a public map layer showing active construction permits, a long-requested tool for neighbors tired of surprise dumpsters.

The layer updates nightly from the permit system and includes type, status, and a link to scanned documents when available. It went live at 6 a.m. on a Tuesday with no announcement beyond a line in the department’s newsletter, which is how 3,100 people found it in the first week and how planning analyst Ruth Cho ended up answering email until eight that evening.

There are 1,847 active permits on the map. Historical permits before 2018 remain incomplete, because that is when the department migrated off a system that stored addresses as free text. Everything older exists on microfilm and in eleven filing cabinets on the third floor, and Cho is careful not to promise it. “We are not going to retro-key thirty years of handwriting,” she said. “If anyone tells you that is on a roadmap, ask them for the line item.”

> Community groups immediately found errors - a demolished garage still listed as active, a major renovation missing entirely.

The garage, on Threadneedle Lane, came down in 2022. Its permit was never closed out because the final inspection was signed on paper and the paper never made it back to a keyboard. The missing renovation was a $2.4 million conversion on the old cannery block, absent because the applicant filed under a parcel number that had been split in a lot-line adjustment and never reconciled.

Staff say a feedback form is the intended fix path, not informal emails. It is a five-field form, and Cho’s team has committed to a ten-working-day response. In the first month they received 340 reports. About 190 were genuine data errors, roughly 90 were duplicates of the same nine problems, and about 60 were complaints about construction noise that had wandered into the wrong channel entirely.

## The error rate is the point, or so the department says

The Cannery Block Neighbors, who found the missing renovation within about forty minutes of launch, are the layer’s most active users and its most persistent critics. Their volunteer coordinator, Aurelio Manx, keeps a spreadsheet of every discrepancy the group has filed - 61 to date, 44 corrected.

Manx supports the layer and is unwilling to call the error rate a triumph of transparency. “The department is framing this as crowdsourcing,” he said. “It is unpaid labor. My members are retirees doing quality assurance on a municipal database for free, and when we find something, the fix takes two weeks. If a private company shipped data this dirty, nobody would say the customers finding the bugs was the plan.”

Cho does not entirely disagree, and her rebuttal is a budget argument rather than a philosophical one. Cleaning the data before release would have cost, by her estimate, eighteen months and two full-time positions the department does not have. “The choice was never clean-and-public versus dirty-and-public,” she said. “It was dirty-and-public versus invisible. It has been invisible since 1994. Somebody had to eat the embarrassment.”

Developers are less enthusiastic about competitors seeing timelines, but the data was always public; it was only inconvenient. “Sunlight was always the policy,” Cho said. “Now it has a basemap.”

That inconvenience had a price: a records request for the same information cost $0.35 a page and took, on average, nine days. One local title researcher told the department she had spent roughly $4,000 a year on requests that now cost nothing, and asked, reasonably, whether she should be pleased or worried.

## What comes next, and what the lawyers said

A second layer for sidewalk closures is planned after the sensor network contract completes its first phase. That contract - $1.6 million over three years - covers curb-line sensors on eleven corridors, and its first phase is scheduled to close in September.

Vendors outside the winning bid have already begun marketing “complementary” modules to individual departments. Procurement lawyers warned departments not to sole-source add-ons that re-create the original competition in miniature, and issued a two-page memo in January after a public works manager was pitched a $190,000 analytics package described in the sales deck as “an extension, not a procurement.”

The open export clause in the base contract is the provision Cho points to most often. Every dataset the city puts into the vendor’s system must be exportable in a documented, non-proprietary format on demand, and the city retains the right to publish it. Whether the architecture holds under political pressure will matter more than the launch press release - an export clause only helps if somebody keeps exercising it, and Cho has asked her team to run a full export quarterly whether anyone needs it or not.

Security reviewers asked for independent penetration tests before any camera expansion. The city agreed to publish a redacted summary of findings, a compromise that satisfied neither the most open advocates nor the most cautious staff.

Manx has a smaller ask, and it is the one still unanswered. He wants the map to show the date each record was last verified by a human, not just the nightly sync timestamp. The department has said it is technically possible. It has not said when.

He mentions it every month at the planning commission’s public comment period, three minutes at a time. He has been doing this since March. He says he will keep doing it until either the field appears or somebody explains, on the record, why it cannot.
