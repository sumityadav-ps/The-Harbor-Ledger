Between July 27 and August 4, Port Meridian recorded nine consecutive days above 94 degrees and four nights that never dropped below 78. Meridian General’s emergency department saw 1,847 patients over that stretch against a summer baseline of about 1,150.

The hospital did not divert a single ambulance. Chief nursing officer Ardith Ferrell will tell you the reason, and it is not a story about capacity planning. It is a story about 340 hours of mandatory overtime and a staffing agency invoice that has not yet been reconciled.

“We got through it on people’s Saturdays,” Ferrell said. “That is a strategy you can run once. We have now run it twice.”

## What came through the door

Heat-specific presentations — heat exhaustion, heat stroke, rhabdomyolysis — accounted for 211 visits, or about 11 percent of the surge. That number surprises people who expect heat to arrive as heat.

The larger share arrived as decompensation of something else. Cardiac and respiratory presentations rose 34 percent. Dialysis patients who missed a session because transport was delayed or a family member would not drive in the afternoon. Psychiatric holds up 41 percent, which Ferrell attributes partly to medication effects — several common antipsychotics impair thermoregulation — and partly to the collapse of routine when a person’s apartment is 96 degrees at midnight.

The single largest identifiable cohort was people over 70 living alone in buildings without central cooling. Meridian General logged 288 such admissions across the nine days. Of those, 94 came from six buildings, all of them on the channel flats, all of them built before 1972, and four of them senior housing.

The volunteer heat sensor grid, now in its second summer, had flagged those blocks in both years. The porch sensor on Lowline Avenue read a nighttime minimum of 84.1 degrees on August 1, six degrees above the airport station.

> A building that does not cool down at night is a medical device that has been switched off, and nobody inspects it.

## The cooling network did not work

The county opened nine cooling centers. Total attendance across nine days was 2,340 visits, which sounds respectable until it is set against the 14,000 households the health department estimates lack functional cooling.

Deputy Health Officer Adaeze Marsh, who ran the after-action review on last spring’s smoke episode and is now running this one, has a preliminary explanation and does not like it.

Six of the nine centers closed at 6 p.m. The heat index in Port Meridian peaked between 4 and 7 p.m. on seven of the nine days and did not fall below the department’s own danger threshold until after 10 p.m. on four of them. Two centers had no accessible restroom. None permitted pets, which Marsh says is the single most cited reason in the department’s exit surveys for people who declined transport — cited more often than distance.

“We built a network for a heat wave that ends at dinner,” she said. “That heat wave does not exist.”

The transport contract expired in June and was extended on a month-to-month basis at the previous rate. Ride volume ran 22 percent below the equivalent 2025 episode despite hotter conditions. The department has asked the vendor for dispatch logs.

## The staffing number

The harder problem is that the county’s environmental health division is authorized for 31 positions and is currently filled at 19.

Six of the twelve vacancies are community health workers — the people who make the reminder calls, run the door-knock canvass in flagged buildings, and staff the centers. The posted wage is $19.40 an hour. Marsh’s division has hired eleven community health workers since January 2025 and lost nine, six of them to Meridian General and two to the school district, both of which pay more for adjacent work.

During the nine days, the door-knock canvass reached 610 of a target list of 2,400 addresses. In 2024, with the division at 27 of 31, it reached 2,100 of 2,600.

“I can tell you exactly what those 1,800 unvisited doors cost, because I can tell you how many of those addresses showed up in the hospital’s admission list,” Marsh said. She would not give the number on the record before the review is finalized. She did say the word for it was “not small.”

The health board has a supplemental request in front of it for $640,000: a wage floor of $24.50 for community health workers, four additional positions, and money to keep three centers open until 10 p.m. It goes to the county council on September 9.

## The argument against

Council member Pell Vantry, who chairs the budget committee, is not opposed to the wage floor and is opposed to the way it is being sold.

“Every department that gets a bad summer comes here in September with a number and a body count,” Vantry said. “I have approved eleven of those in six years. Meanwhile the reason those six buildings on the flats are 84 degrees at midnight is that we have a building code that requires heating and does not require cooling, and nobody in this room wants that fight because it costs landlords money instead of costing the general fund money.”

His counterproposal is to fund the wage floor at half and spend the remainder drafting a habitable-temperature ordinance requiring a cooled room in residential buildings over a certain size, phased over eight years.

Marsh’s response was that she agrees with the ordinance and that halving her staffing request to fund it means the canvass fails again next July, before any code takes effect in 2034. Vantry said that was probably true.

Tenant organizer Leah Fonseca, who has worked the flats for six years, backs both and thinks the ordinance is the only one that lasts. “Adaeze’s people knock on my neighbors’ doors and they are wonderful and they are gone in fourteen months,” she said. “The building is still the building.”

## After

Ferrell has changed one thing at the hospital that required no budget approval. Meridian General now flags every discharged patient whose home address falls in a sensor-identified hot block and holds them for a follow-up call within 48 hours instead of the standard seven days.

It caught 31 people in the week after the heat broke. Nine were readmitted anyway. Ferrell keeps the list of the other twenty-two in a drawer and has said she will bring it to the September 9 hearing if anyone asks her to testify, and probably if they do not.
