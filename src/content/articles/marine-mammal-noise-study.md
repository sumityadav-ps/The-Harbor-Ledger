A multi-year acoustic study suggests harbor porpoises avoid segments of the channel during nights with elevated low-frequency noise, even when daytime averages look moderate.

Lead author Dr. Tess Morin said operational timing may be a sharper tool than blanket caps that are hard to enforce. “The animals are telling us about the schedule,” she said.

Field technicians emphasized that sensors fail in ordinary ways: bird strikes, battery sag, firmware quirks. The science depends as much on maintenance schedules as on elegant models.

Students on the project are writing methods sections that double as training manuals for the next cohort. Continuity is a research output when grants turn over every two years.

Public communication remains a deliberate practice. Figures are released with uncertainty bands, and press officers have stopped promising single-number headlines the data cannot support.

The study used both fixed hydrophones and boat-based surveys. Data and code are public, an insistence from funders tired of inaccessible coastal science.

A working group will explore voluntary quiet windows during peak presence months. Environmental groups want those windows written into permits; industry wants pilots first.

Funders will decide the next phase on a mix of metrics and narrative. The team’s wager is that honest mixed results travel farther than glossy overclaim.

Replication plans are already in motion with a partner lab inland. Independent confirmation is the price of any municipal interest in the work, researchers said.

People closest to the story keep returning to a practical question: what changes on Monday morning. Titles and press notes matter less than schedules, budgets, and who answers the phone.
