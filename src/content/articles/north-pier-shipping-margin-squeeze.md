North Pier Shipping said Friday it will suspend three scheduled coastal routes beginning in September, a move executives framed as temporary discipline after fuel hedge contracts rolled over at sharply higher rates.

The company, based in Port Meridian's industrial basin, moves containerized goods between mid-coast terminals and inland railheads. In a note to customers, chief operating officer Rhea Lang confirmed that the Grey Harbor, Seacliff, and South Bend legs would pause "until unit economics stabilize."

Together the three legs account for roughly 18 percent of North Pier's sailings and about 11 percent of its revenue, according to figures the company circulated to its customer list. The imbalance is the point: they are the cheapest freight the company carries, booked at rates negotiated in 2023, and they are the legs that burn the most fuel per container because they call at shallow berths that require slow approaches and tug assists.

The hedge that expired on June 30 covered about 70 percent of North Pier's bunker fuel at a fixed rate. Its replacement, signed in a market that has moved against buyers for four consecutive quarters, covers 55 percent at a rate Lang described only as "materially higher." Two brokers familiar with the mid-coast market, speaking on background because they broker for both North Pier and its competitors, put the increase between 22 and 30 percent.

> Labor leaders reacted cautiously.

"We are not hearing mass layoffs yet, but idle ships mean idle hours," said Kellan Morse of the Channel Dockworkers Guild. Morse said the guild's concern is less about headcount than about the shift structure that sits underneath it: the Grey Harbor rotation supports two gangs that also cover Tuesday and Thursday overflow at the main terminal. Pull the rotation and the overflow coverage becomes voluntary overtime nobody is guaranteed.

Warehouse operators further inland reported they were already shifting volume to trucking consortia, which could reverse last year's gains in waterborne share. That share climbed from 34 to 41 percent between 2024 and 2025, a number the regional freight council has used repeatedly in grant applications.

Customers, for their part, are rewriting delivery windows rather than switching vendors overnight. Switching costs remain high when cold-chain or hazmat certifications are involved, which buys time for the company even as goodwill thins.

## The counter-case

Not everyone reads the announcement as retreat. Talia Bergsen, who ran scheduling at a competing operator for eleven years before moving to consulting, argues that North Pier is doing the thing its peers have avoided.

"Everybody on this coast is carrying at least one leg that loses money on every sailing and stays on the board because canceling it is embarrassing," Bergsen said. "North Pier just took the embarrassment. If the hedge math is real, the alternative was to keep those three legs and start missing covenants by spring."

Her argument has a second half that is harder for the waterfront to hear. Bergsen contends the Seacliff berth in particular has been subsidized for a decade by the rest of the network, and that a genuinely stabilized North Pier might never restore it. "Temporary is a word you use in July," she said.

Municipal economic staff said they were not preparing emergency aid, but they were updating dashboards that track dock employment and overtime hours. "We measure first, then talk about tools," one analyst said.

A second-order effect is credit. Trade insurers have not raised premiums yet, though brokers report more questions about concentration risk among mid-size shippers who depend on a single coastal operator.

Analysts at Kelp & Harbor Research noted that North Pier's debt load remains manageable, but free cash flow turned negative in the second quarter for the first time since 2021. "This is a margin story, not a demand story," wrote principal analyst Jun Park. "The docks are busy. The contracts are wrong."

Park's note put the second-quarter free cash flow figure at negative $4.1 million against $2.2 million positive in the same quarter last year, and estimated that the three suspended legs, if the announced savings hold, close roughly two-thirds of that gap. The remaining third is the part nobody has explained.

Mayor's office officials declined to say whether municipal port fees would be reviewed. A spokesperson said only that the city "remains committed to a diversified freight corridor."

For retailers who relied on the cancelled legs for midweek replenishment, the practical effect arrives fast. Several independent grocers told The Harbor Ledger they had already lengthened order cycles by two days. "We plan around the ship, not the other way around," said Delia Crowe of Crowe & Sons Market.

Crowe has run the market on Bellweather Street for nineteen years and keeps her order calendar on a whiteboard behind the register, columns ruled in permanent marker, entries in dry-erase. On Thursday she erased the Wednesday column entirely and redrew it as a Friday column, then stood back and looked at it for a while.

"Two days sounds like nothing," she said. "Two days is soft fruit."

North Pier will host a customer briefing next Tuesday. Investors will look for a timeline, not another promise that the pause is temporary. Lang has said the company will address restoration criteria at the briefing; she has not said whether those criteria will be numeric.

Industry peers watched the announcement carefully. Similar firms along the corridor have been quiet about their own cost structures, but private conversations suggest several are modeling contingency schedules for the fourth quarter.

In the basin, the practical arithmetic is already underway. The Grey Harbor gang keeps a rotation sheet pinned inside the dispatch shed door, ruled by hand, September running down the left margin. Morse said someone crossed out the fourteenth and the twenty-first this week and left the rest of the month alone.

"Nobody's touching October," he said. "October is where people are still pretending."
