An inland farm-stay network expanded to eighteen properties, pairing overnight lodging with optional farm activities under clearer rules about what is experience and what is work.

The Ridgeback Valley Farm Stay Association added six farms this winter, up from twelve, spread across the drainages east of Port Meridian between the Callow River and the county line. Together the members offer 61 guest rooms and eleven cabins — small numbers by hotel standards, but roughly a third of all lodging inventory in a valley with no hotel at all.

Earlier complaints involved guests feeling pressured to labor unpaid. The new charter requires written opt-in tasks, time limits, and the right to pure leisure without guilt.

> Hosts say the clarity helps.

## What the charter actually says

The document runs to four pages, which association coordinator Maeve Torrance describes, without embarrassment, as three pages longer than anyone wanted.

Its core provisions: any farm activity offered to guests must be listed in advance with an honest time estimate; guests sign for each task individually rather than agreeing to a blanket "farm participation" clause at booking; no single activity may exceed three hours; and no host may condition any part of the stay — meals, rate, checkout time — on participation. A separate clause bars hosts from asking guests to operate machinery, handle livestock unsupervised, or work above ground level.

The rules came out of eleven written complaints filed with the association in 2024 and 2025. Torrance read a representative one aloud at the charter's adoption meeting, with the guest's permission and name removed: a couple who had booked what they understood to be a quiet weekend and spent six hours of it moving irrigation pipe because, as the guest wrote, "it seemed rude not to."

"We want ambassadors, not free harvest crews," said orchardist Dana Will, who chaired the drafting group and whose farm above Callow Bend now posts a laminated card in each room listing the week's optional tasks with times: cider pressing, 90 minutes; grafting demonstration, 45; nothing at all, unlimited.

## The dissent from the small operators

Not every member signed happily. Two of the smallest properties — both single-cabin operations run by couples in their sixties — argued the charter imports employment-law formality into what had been hospitality.

Bertrand Oyelaran, who runs a two-room stay on a nine-acre goat dairy, was the most vocal holdout and eventually signed anyway.

"My guests come into the milking parlor because they are curious and I like the company," he said. "Now there is a form. The form does not make me a better host. It makes me a smaller business with a filing cabinet." His practical complaint is that the paperwork falls hardest on operators with no staff to do it — the same operators the charter's protections were least aimed at, since none of the eleven complaints involved a property under twenty acres.

Torrance's response is that a voluntary standard with holes is not a standard, and that the association negotiated the three-hour cap and the individual sign-offs specifically so that a farm the size of Oyelaran's could comply with a single card and a pen. The association has since produced a one-page short form for properties with fewer than four rooms, adopted in February.

## Bookings, blossom, and the shuttle problem

Bookings rise in weeks tied to blossom and crush events. The association's aggregate occupancy last year ran 84 percent across the blossom fortnight in late April and 79 percent during the September crush, against 31 percent for the year as a whole — a spread that explains why nearly every member still holds a second job or a primary farm income.

Transport remains the friction: car-free travelers need shuttle coordination that only a few farms offer. Four members currently meet guests at the Meridian bus terminal, an unpaid hour each way. A pilot van service — one 12-seat vehicle, Fridays and Sundays, $14 a seat — is budgeted at $31,000 for a trial season and remains $9,000 short.

A shared calendar prevents every property from hosting large dinners on the same Saturday and starving the labor pool. The valley has perhaps forty people who reliably work event kitchens, and in October 2024 three farms scheduled harvest dinners on the same night and two of them ended up cooking for sixty guests with the host family and one hired hand.

Operators are training staff to sell shoulder season without apologizing for closed kitchens. The script emphasizes light, space, and walking, not a thinner version of peak week. Review scores mention silence as often as scenery, which has quietly reshaped room assignments: two members have stopped booking their road-facing rooms entirely in the off months rather than take the one-star reviews.

If the trend holds, shoulder inventory may need more year-round workers rather than purely seasonal hires — a labor puzzle destinations are only beginning to price honestly. Torrance estimated that going from seasonal to year-round staffing at a mid-size member would add roughly $46,000 in annual payroll against maybe $60,000 in additional shoulder revenue, assuming the shoulder revenue shows up.

Local guides are packaging half-day walks that end before dark, a practical design that reduces stranded visitors and emergency calls. Two farms now hand departing walkers a printed card with the last bus time circled in red.

At Will's place, the laminated card in the guest rooms has one more line at the bottom, added after the charter passed and not required by it: "If you would rather sit on the porch, that is also what this farm is for." She said a guest last October crossed out "porch" and wrote "hayloft," which is not on the list of approved locations, and which she has decided not to report to the association.
