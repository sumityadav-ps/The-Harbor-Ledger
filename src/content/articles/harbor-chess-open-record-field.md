The Harbor Chess Open posted a record field this year, forcing organizers to extend round times and add a second hall for scholastic sections.

Entries closed at 1,046, up from 712 last February and 388 the year the tournament moved out of the library basement. Of those, 611 are under eighteen. The scholastic sections alone would have been the largest chess event ever held in Port Meridian if they had been staged on their own.

Top boards feature several international masters and a growing cohort of online-trained teenagers who treat classical time controls as a second language.

> Volunteers rebuilt pairings software after last year’s bottleneck.

“We learned that growth without logistics is just a longer queue,” said chief arbiter Helen Cho.

Cho is precise about what went wrong in 2025. The tournament ran a single pairing station on one laptop, and each round required a full re-sort after late withdrawals were entered by hand. Round three began ninety-four minutes behind schedule. By round five the delay had compounded to two hours and eleven minutes, and the hall’s lease ended at eleven o’clock whether or not anybody had finished. Fourteen games that night were adjudicated rather than played out.

This year four volunteers — three of them programmers at a logistics firm on the west quay, one a retired schools inspector — spent nine months rewriting the pairing pipeline. Withdrawals now come in from a tablet at the door and propagate before the sort runs. Results are entered at the board on scoresheets with a scannable code, a change Cho pushed for after watching a runner carry paper across 200 tables.

Round one this year started four minutes late. Cho, who took the arbiter’s chair in 2023 after eleven years as a hospital scheduler, says she stole the design from triage: the constraint is never the table, it is the queue in front of the table.

## Why the rounds still got longer

The fix bought time that organizers immediately spent. Time control moved from 90 minutes plus a 30-second increment to 100 minutes plus 30, adding roughly forty minutes to the outer edge of a long game. That was a deliberate concession to the top section, where several titled players had complained that the shorter control made the event a poor rating investment relative to travel.

Not everyone welcomed it. Vela Ostrow, who has run the city’s junior club out of a community hall on Ferrand Street for two decades, argued against the change at the organizing meeting in October and lost the vote 6-3.

“A ten-year-old at board 180 is playing the same clock as a grandmaster at board one, and nobody at board one has to be collected by a parent who finishes a shift at nine,” Ostrow said. “We extended the day for the twenty people the event is written about, and the six hundred it is actually for absorbed it.” She has proposed splitting the scholastic sections onto a separate, shorter control next year. Cho says the idea is on the agenda and that she is not yet persuaded, because two controls in one building means two round schedules, two sets of arbiters, and the reintroduction of exactly the coordination problem the rewrite removed.

## The room

The second hall — Exhibition Room C, borrowed from a cancelled boat show — holds 340 boards under a ceiling of exposed ductwork. Foam baffles hung along the north wall cut the ring of the clocks; a volunteer measured 41 decibels at the center of the room during round four, quieter than the corridor outside.

Organizers spent 3,200 on those baffles and consider it the single best line in a budget of 68,000. Entry fees cover about 71 percent of that. A hardware distributor on Kestrel Street underwrites the prize fund, which pays 4,000 for first in the open section and, for the first time this year, an equal 4,000 split across the top three under-14 finishers.

Local cafes near the venue report a weekend spike in quiet tables occupied by people staring at pocket boards between rounds. The Tern, a coffee house two doors from the loading entrance, put out a sandwich board on Saturday reading NO WIFI, PLENTY OF CHAIRS, and its owner said it was the busiest non-summer weekend she has recorded.

There is an accessibility question the organizers have not solved. Room C has one lift, and it was out of service for six hours on Saturday afternoon. Three players in the under-12 section were carried, with their families’ consent, up a service stair by volunteers. Cho called it “not acceptable and entirely foreseeable” and said the 2027 venue contract will not be signed without a redundancy clause.

By late Sunday the top boards had thinned to four games, and the hall had gone from a thousand people to perhaps sixty, most of them standing in a loose ring at a polite distance. On board two a fourteen-year-old from the Basin district, unrated eighteen months ago, held a rook endgame against an international master for eighty-one moves before offering a draw with both hands flat on the table. The master accepted, then spent twenty minutes showing her where, on move fifty-three, she had missed the win.
