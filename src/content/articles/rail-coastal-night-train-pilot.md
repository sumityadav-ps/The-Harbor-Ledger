A pilot coastal night train between Port Meridian and the southern capital sold out its first month of berths, surprising even the regional rail authority that fought for the experiment.

All 1,116 berths across the first thirty-one departures went in eleven days. The authority had modeled a 55 percent load factor for the pilot period and set the fare — $94 for a shared four-berth compartment, $168 for a single — against that assumption. It has not adjusted the fare, a decision that has already produced a small internal argument about whether the sellout proves demand or proves underpricing.

Cars are refurbished, not luxurious. The three sleepers were built in 1988, ran overnight service until 2007, and spent thirteen years in a yard outside Tallow Bend under tarpaulins that failed sometime around 2016. Refurbishment cost $4.3 million and did not include air conditioning replacement, which is why the authority scheduled the pilot for spring and autumn only.

The pitch is simple: leave after dinner, sleep, arrive downtown without an airport shuffle. Departure is 9:40 p.m., arrival 6:55 a.m. Early riders praised the quiet cars and criticized the breakfast box.

> On-time performance will decide the pilot's fate.

Through the first month the service arrived within fifteen minutes of schedule on 22 of 31 nights. Four arrivals ran more than an hour late. The worst, on the night of May 19, reached the southern capital at 9:12 a.m., two hours and seventeen minutes down, after holding twice at the river junction.

Freight interference remains the known risk on shared track south of the river junction. The night train has dispatch priority on paper. In practice, priority is exercised by a dispatcher balancing a passenger consist against a 9,000-foot unit train that cannot be held on a siding it will not fit in.

"Everybody writes the priority rule and nobody writes the siding lengths," said Ilse Varga, the rail authority's operations planner. "We have four places between the junction and Tallow Bend where a long freight physically cannot clear the main. On those nights we are not being disrespected. We are being geometry."

## What riders actually said

The authority's post-trip survey went to all first-month riders and came back with a 43 percent response rate, unusually high, which the authority reads as enthusiasm and one board member reads as complaint energy.

Mean satisfaction was 4.2 of 5. Sleep quality scored 3.8. The breakfast box scored 2.1, the lowest figure in any category the authority has surveyed in six years, and drew 94 free-text comments, of which the operations team has taped one to the pantry door in the crew car.

"They gave me a shrink-wrapped roll and a fruit cup at six in the morning after charging me a hundred and sixty-eight dollars," wrote one rider, quoted with permission in the survey appendix. "I have had better breakfasts in a hospital, and the hospital was not optional."

Varga said the box will be replaced in the second pilot block with hot service in the lounge car, at a cost the authority puts at $6.40 per rider against $2.15 now.

Quiet enforcement was the surprise success. The authority designated one of three sleepers as a silent car with no phone calls after 10 p.m., expecting to sell it last. It sold first on 28 of 31 nights.

## The case against continuing

Not everyone reads a sellout as vindication. Board member Teodor Rasch, who voted against the pilot in 2024 and has not changed his position, argues the numbers say something the authority does not want to hear.

"Eleven hundred berths is nothing," Rasch said. "The same corridor moves eleven hundred people on a Tuesday afternoon on trains that already exist. We spent four point three million dollars and a dispatch slot that costs freight customers real money so that thirty-six people a night can have an experience. I like the experience. I am asking whether an authority with a bus replacement backlog should be buying experiences."

Rasch's sharper point concerns the dispatch slot. Every night the sleeper runs, a freight operator absorbs a hold, and those operators pay access charges that fund the corridor. He argues the true cost of the pilot has never been calculated because nobody has priced the holds.

Varga does not dismiss this. She said the authority has begun logging hold minutes attributable to the night train and will publish them with the second-block results — 431 minutes in the first month, a figure Rasch called "the first honest number in the file."

Hotels in both cities report slight shifts in check-in patterns on train days, with more 8 a.m. early check-ins requested on Thursdays and Sundays. Tourism boards are packaging museum tickets with berths for shoulder months.

If metrics hold, a second frequency could arrive next year. If not, the cars may revert to daytime excursion service — a quieter failure than a loud cancellation.

The second pilot block opens for booking on September 8, thirty-two departures, same fare. Varga said the authority will decide the service's future against three numbers: on-time arrival above 85 percent, load factor above 70 percent, and hold minutes below 400 a month. It currently meets one of the three.

The crew car keeps a running board — a whiteboard where the night conductor writes the arrival time in minutes off schedule, one line per night, no commentary. Twenty-two of the entries are single digits. One says 137, underlined, and next to it somebody has drawn a small locomotive with too many wheels.
