At 6:40 on Saturday evening, in the long room at Net Loft Gallery, a woman named Perpetua Sandhu stood on a stepladder with a wooden spoon and hit a hanging brass rudder blade three times. It is the largest piece in the gallery’s current show, it weighs 61 pounds, and it is not supposed to be struck.

The artist, Konrad Vye, had cleared it that afternoon. “It was a rudder,” he said. “It spent thirty years being hit by water.”

Ninety-six people sat down at one table running the length of the room for the second Late Harvest supper, a collaboration between the gallery and Basin Kitchen that began last year as a way to fill an August that neither institution had a plan for. Tickets were $110, sold out in nine minutes, and lost money.

## The menu that did not happen

Basin Kitchen’s chef, Odalys Ferrer, wrote the first draft of the menu in May. It had five tomato preparations on it. By the second week of August she had thrown all five out.

The reason is documented in the harvest logs of the four farms Ferrer buys from on the ridge. The nine-day heat stretch that ended August 4 pushed daytime highs past 94 during the crucial ripening window, and tomatoes largely stop setting and coloring above about 90. Two farms lost the mid-season crop outright. Rell Farms, on the Vessey Road side, reported 38 percent of expected volume, three weeks late.

“You do not get to be sad about it in front of the guests,” Ferrer said. “But you do not get to lie either. If I put four tomatoes on a plate in a year like this, that is a fantasy, and the person who grew them is sitting at table six.”

What she served instead was structured around what the heat did produce. Peppers loved it — a chilled roasted pepper and almond soup opened the meal, poured tableside from copper jugs. The second course was cucumber, which came in early and abundant, cut thick and dressed with a two-year koji from the fermentation program that Basin Kitchen has been running since the spring Fermentation Week.

The tomatoes appeared once, in the fourth course, in a quantity that read as deliberate. Six slices, one shared per pair of guests, from the first Rell picking, which came off the vine on Thursday.

> Six slices of tomato on a Saturday in August is not scarcity theater, it is a receipt.

## The room

Net Loft reopened last year after its rebuild, and the long room still has the loading doors that give onto the water. They were open. The harbor noise arrived in the gaps between courses, along with, at one point, a departing ferry horn that stopped conversation for four seconds and then produced a round of applause.

Vye’s show, “Recovered Gear,” is eleven pieces built from salvaged marine hardware — the rudder blade, a winch drum, a run of chain suspended in a catenary that hangs eleven feet over the middle of the table. Gallery director Roshan Mehl said the insurance conversation about seating people under 400 pounds of suspended steel took six weeks and required an engineer’s letter.

“The underwriter asked me to describe the failure mode,” Mehl said. “I said there isn’t one, and he said write that down and sign it, and I understood his position.”

The lighting was the concession. Vye wanted the pieces lit from below, as they had been all summer. The supper needed light on the plates. The compromise ran warm downlights on the table and left the sculptures in the shadow they had been designed against, which Vye called “wrong, and fine,” and which several guests said made the room feel like a hold rather than a gallery.

## The economics, which are bad

Mehl is candid that Late Harvest does not pay for itself. Ticket revenue was $10,560. Direct costs — food, twelve hired servers, the engineer’s letter, additional insurance rider, tent rental for the prep area in the alley — came to $13,900. The gallery covered the gap from its programming reserve.

“It is a membership event that is priced like a dinner and behaves like a fundraiser that does not raise funds,” Mehl said. “The board has asked me about it twice.”

Her argument for keeping it is that the event does something the gallery’s exhibition calendar cannot: it puts 96 people in the building for three hours who are not there to look at art, and roughly a third of them have never been inside before. Nineteen memberships were sold at the door on Saturday, which is more than the gallery sold in the previous four months combined.

The counter-argument came from a board member who said it in front of the room, at the microphone, during the thank-yous, which is either bad governance or unusually good transparency depending on who is describing it.

Cyrus Oleander, board treasurer, said he had voted against the event both years and would vote against it a third time. “We have a capital reserve that will not cover a roof,” he said. “This building is on the water. I love this dinner. I have been to both of them. I am also the person who will have to tell you in four years why the north gallery is closed, and I would like it on the record that I said the words ‘endowment’ and ‘roof’ while everyone was in a good mood.” He got a laugh and, from a smaller portion of the room, actual applause.

Mehl said afterward that Oleander is not wrong and that she has stopped arguing with him about whether the reserve matters. Her position is that a gallery with a perfect roof and no visitors has solved the wrong problem, and that they will have this fight again in June.

## What ended it

Dessert was a plum and thyme tart, plums being the other thing the heat was good for, and after it Ferrer’s staff cleared the length of the table in about eleven minutes.

Sandhu, who had struck the rudder to open the meal, came back up the stepladder at 10:15 to close it. Vye stopped her. He asked whether anyone at the table had never made a loud noise on purpose in a room full of strangers, and eventually a fourteen-year-old named Bettina Roscoe, at table four, put her hand up. She got the spoon.

She hit it once, too hard, and the note went on for a very long time.
