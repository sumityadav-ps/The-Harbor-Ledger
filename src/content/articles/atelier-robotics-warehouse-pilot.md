Atelier Robotics began a six-month pilot of assistive sorting arms at two Port Meridian fulfillment sites, with union observers on site for the first week of operation.

Eight arms are running at the Fell Street cross-dock and six at the smaller Anchor Way facility, mounted on rails above the induction line rather than bolted to the floor. They are painted a flat institutional grey that one picker described, accurately, as the color of a filing cabinet. They move less dramatically than the promotional video suggests: a reach, a pause of perhaps two seconds while the vision system resolves the carton, then a swing of about forty degrees onto the outbound belt.

The arms handle repetitive mid-weight lifts between conveyors - the eight-to-twenty-kilogram range that Atelier’s own study identified as the source of a disproportionate share of shoulder and lower-back claims. Human workers still manage exceptions, packing quality, and anything irregular. “If it looks weird, a person still decides,” said site lead Tomas Reed. “Crushed corner, wet seam, something rattling that shouldn’t. The arm doesn’t have an opinion about a rattle.”

> Safety protocols required new floor markings and a kill-switch culture that supervisors say is stricter than the vendor’s default.

Atelier shipped the units with two emergency stops per rail. Fell Street added four more, at knee height, and rewrote the training so that hitting one is never treated as an incident requiring explanation. Reed said that was the single most argued-over decision in the whole install.

“The vendor’s posture is that a stop costs throughput, so you document why you stopped,” he said. “Do that and within a month nobody stops. We flipped it. You stop, you say nothing, you get a coffee. I would rather eat forty false stops than one real one I have to call somebody’s wife about.”

The Fell Street line logged 96 stops in the first three weeks. Four were, in the safety officer’s classification, genuine near-misses. The rest were people being careful, which is the number Reed says he is watching.

## What the union got in writing

Local 214, which represents roughly 340 workers across both sites, did not oppose the pilot. It also did not sign a friendly letter about it. What it did was spend eleven weeks negotiating a four-page side agreement, and business agent Yolanda Petrescu is blunt that the paper matters more than the machines.

Atelier’s contract with both sites freezes full-time roles for the pilot duration and funds retraining hours - 40 hours per affected worker, paid at rate, covering arm supervision, fault diagnosis, and the vendor’s maintenance certification. Injury rates will be published monthly in aggregated form, broken out by site and by shift, to a joint committee with two union seats.

And there is the exit clause. If metrics hold, a third site could join in winter. If not, the arms leave - a clause workers insisted remain in plain text, not incorporated by reference into an appendix. Petrescu read it aloud at the ratification meeting because, she said, half the room had been through a “pilot” in 2021 that quietly became permanent when nobody could find the paragraph that said otherwise.

## The argument that has not been settled

Skeptics worry the pilot is a soft on-ramp to headcount cuts, and the strongest version of that worry does not come from outside the building.

Dessie Ferrand has worked induction at Fell Street for nine years. She likes the arms. She also does not believe the freeze protects anyone past November. “Nobody is going to fire me,” she said, sorting a bin of returns during a break she was technically not taking. “They are going to not hire the two people who quit in October. That is not in the agreement. That has never been in anybody’s agreement.”

Attrition backfilling is, in fact, not addressed in the side letter. Petrescu acknowledges it. She says the union traded it for the injury data, on the theory that a public monthly number is a lever you can still pull in three years, and a hiring guarantee for six months is not.

Atelier’s regional director, Sunil Achterberg, declined to promise headcount parity beyond the pilot, which at least has the virtue of honesty. He argued instead that the arms make the sites viable at all. “The alternative on this waterfront is not more jobs. The alternative is the volume moving to a purpose-built facility ninety minutes inland where there is no local, no side letter, and no reporter in the break room.”

Nobody at the ratification meeting found that comforting. Several people found it plausible, which was worse.

The first joint committee report is due May 12. Reed has already told his supervisors what he expects the number to look like: injury claims will probably not move much in the first quarter, because the shoulders that are going to fail this year were being worn out in 2019. The arms cannot un-lift anything.

On the floor at shift change, the practical verdict is quieter than either side’s framing. A picker named Idris Vann, twenty-two, said the arms are fine, boring, slightly too slow on wet cartons. Then he mentioned, without being asked, that he has stopped taking two ibuprofen before his Thursday shift. He has not needed to since February.
