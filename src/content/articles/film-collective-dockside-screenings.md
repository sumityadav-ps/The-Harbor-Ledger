The Dockside Film Collective returns this summer with outdoor screenings powered by shore electricity and a sound system tuned to keep dialogue intelligible without rattling apartment windows.

The nine-night series will run Thursdays from late June through August on the concrete apron of Pier 4, the same working quay the collective has borrowed since 2018, with capacity set at 320 seated and a standing overflow the fire marshal caps at 60.

Last year's generator complaints nearly ended the series. Organizers spent the off-season fundraising for a quieter technical plot and a written agreement with the neighborhood association.

> Programming mixes restorations, local shorts, and one "audience pick" night decided by ranked-choice ballot at the library.

## What the generators cost

The 2025 season produced 37 formal noise complaints to the city, 31 of them from the four residential conversions on the landward side of Dockside Street. Nearly all named the two diesel generators, which sat behind a plywood baffle and ran from roughly 7 p.m. until the last cable came down near midnight.

The city's noise officer measured 68 decibels at the nearest residential façade during a July screening — below the enforcement threshold for a permitted event, but, as the officer's memo noted dryly, sustained for five hours at a frequency that plywood does nothing about.

"The film was never the problem," said collective technical director Wren Ashgrove. "You cannot hear a movie from a fourth-floor window with the glass shut. You can hear a diesel engine from anywhere in the neighborhood, including inside a dream."

The fix cost $41,800: a permanent shore-power tie-in at the pier's south bollard, negotiated with the port authority, plus a rebuilt speaker plot. The collective raised $26,000 from a small-grants program, $9,300 from a membership drive, and covered the rest by cancelling a planned second series in the fall.

## The new sound plot

The technical change is less about volume than about geometry. The old system pushed sound from two stacks beside the screen, which meant the back rows needed enough level that the front rows were loud and the buildings behind got the spill.

The rebuilt plot uses eight smaller speakers on delay towers spaced through the seating area, each running about 12 decibels quieter than the old stacks, timed so the sound arrives with the picture rather than after it. Ashgrove's target is 62 decibels at the last row and under 45 at the residential façade — quieter, at the buildings, than the 48 the port's own container handling produces on an ordinary weeknight.

The neighborhood agreement, signed in November by the collective and the Dockside Street Residents' Association, commits the series to a 10:45 p.m. hard stop for amplified sound, a published schedule six weeks in advance, and a named phone number that reaches a person, not a machine, during every screening.

## Not everyone is satisfied

Association secretary Oyin Marchbanks signed the agreement and remains skeptical of it.

"Nine nights is nine nights," she said. "The hard stop is real and I believe them about the speakers. What I do not have is any assurance about what happens the year they get popular and want eighteen nights, or the year somebody else rents the pier and points at this agreement as the precedent for what is allowed here."

Her association pushed unsuccessfully for a cap written into the port's permit rather than into a private agreement between neighbors and an organization that could dissolve. The port declined, saying permit conditions are set season by season.

Ashgrove conceded the point has merit and said the collective would not oppose a permit condition if the port ever proposed one.

## Practical matters

Blankets are encouraged; chairs must stay in marked rows for emergency access — a four-foot lane down the center and two cross-lanes, taped out before every screening and inspected by a fire department walkthrough at 7:30.

Concessions are limited to two vendors who use compostable serviceware, a condition of the port's waste agreement after the 2024 season left the pier's storm drains full of plastic lids. Tickets remain free; the collective passes a bucket and reports an average yield of $2.40 a head.

Captioning is now standard on every title rather than on request, a change made after a 2025 survey found 14 percent of respondents were deaf, hard of hearing, or watching with someone who was. Two accessible seating platforms sit at the center delay towers, where the sound timing is truest.

Programming for the season is not fully announced. Confirmed so far: a restored 1961 documentary about the harbor's last sail-rigged freight run, a night of six local shorts selected from 71 submissions, and the audience-pick ballot, which opens at the Harbor Branch library on May 4.

Opening night is that restored maritime documentary, followed by a Q&A with the surviving editor, Constance Petrusich, now 81 and delighted. Petrusich cut the film over fourteen months in a room above a chandlery two hundred yards from where it will be projected, and she has told organizers she intends to point at the building during the Q&A and would appreciate it if someone aimed a light at it.
