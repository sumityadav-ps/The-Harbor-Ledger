Port Meridian Civic Theatre unveiled a season built entirely around newly commissioned plays by writers based along the coast, a gamble on local voice over brand-name revivals.

Six plays, all premieres, all by writers living between the northern ferry terminus and the river towns three hours south. The commissions run $18,000 each against a house average of $6,500 for a licensed revival - which means the company has committed roughly $108,000 to scripts that do not yet exist, in a building that seats 340.

Artistic director Helen Orth said subscription trends rewarded familiarity, but single-ticket buyers were hungry for work that mentioned their weather. “We decided to stop splitting the difference,” she said. “For nine years we did four safe titles and one risk, and the risk always sold worse and always got the mail. Actual letters, handwritten, about the one play. At some point you have to believe your own audience.”

The numbers behind that decision are less romantic than the framing. Subscriptions have fallen from 1,930 in 2019 to 1,140 this year, while single-ticket sales are up 31 percent over the same period. The subscription base is older, more reliable, and shrinking on a curve the board can extrapolate. Orth’s argument to the board, made over two meetings in November, was that the company can either manage that decline gracefully for a decade or spend the remaining goodwill on something.

> The board approved the season 9-4, and the four dissenters have not gone quiet.

Board member Cordelia Ashgrove-Nunn, who voted no, is happy to explain why. She has served eleven years and helped fundraise the roof.

“I am not the philistine in this story,” she said. “I have read four of the six drafts and two of them are very good. But we are a 340-seat house with fourteen weeks of dark time and $210,000 in reserves, and Helen is asking us to have no known quantity in the calendar at all. One soft title and we are fine. Six soft titles and we are having a different conversation in June about whether the building opens in the autumn. My objection is arithmetic, not taste.”

Orth’s answer is that a revival is not the safe harbor Ashgrove-Nunn believes it is - the last one, a chestnut comedy staged in 2024, ran at 58 percent capacity and lost $11,000. Ashgrove-Nunn counters that it lost a *known* $11,000. Neither has moved the other. Both keep having lunch.

## How it is paid for

Funding comes from a mix of a regional arts fund, a corporate underwriter in shipping logistics, and a novel “scene partner” program where small businesses sponsor individual workshop weeks. A partner puts up $2,400, which covers a week of rehearsal room, two actors, and a stage manager; in return they get their name on the workshop, four tickets, and an open invitation to sit in the room.

Twenty-nine businesses signed on, including a tyre shop, two dentists, and a chandlery that has been on Ropewalk Street since 1948. Development director Anselm Boye-Kittredge admits he expected perhaps twelve and had never seriously modeled the possibility that a tyre shop would want to watch a rehearsal.

Several partners have, in fact, come. The chandler’s owner sat through two afternoons of a scene being reworked and left a note in the stage manager’s book correcting a line about how a mooring cleat is fitted. The line was changed. The playwright, Ines Vartabedian, has said she considers it the best note she got all winter.

Funding remains a patchwork underneath the good story. The underwriter covers lights and insurance; memberships cover programming risk; the regional arts fund grant, $74,000, is confirmed for one year and applied for annually. The board wants an endowment conversation before the next capital surprise, and has wanted one since 2021.

## What the writers were and were not told

Not every play will be about the harbor. Orth was clear: coastal writers are not required to write fog. They are required to premiere here first.

The commissions carry a first-production clause and nothing else - no subject matter, no length, no cast ceiling, which caused mild panic in production when one script arrived with eleven characters. Of the six, two are set in Port Meridian, one in an unnamed inland town, one entirely in a car, one across four decades of a family that never once goes near the water, and one on a ferry, which Orth concedes she should probably have seen coming.

Accessibility upgrades made during the closure - ramps, captioning loops, quieter hours - are now part of the pitch rather than a footnote. Several patrons said they returned because the building finally worked for them. The captioning loop covers every performance, not a designated night, a decision that cost an extra $9,000 and that Orth describes as the least controversial thing she has done in three years.

Student rush tickets expand to weeknights at $9. A post-show conversation series will be moderated by rotating community members rather than only critics; the first four moderators are a harbor pilot, a high school chemistry teacher, a retired nurse, and the chandler.

Rehearsals for the opening production start September 8 in the upstairs room, where the radiator knocks and the window does not close properly against the sound of the loading dock below. Vartabedian has asked that it be left open. She wrote the second act to that noise and says the play does not sound right without it.
