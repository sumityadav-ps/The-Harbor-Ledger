Port Meridian City Council delayed a vote on a transit-oriented housing overlay after a hearing that stretched past midnight and left amendments scattered across a whiteboard.

The overlay would allow mid-rise housing near frequent bus corridors with reduced parking minimums. Supporters called it overdue climate and affordability policy. Opponents warned of shadows, traffic, and speculative teardown pressure.

> Council member Ruth Adeyemi pushed for a narrower map that protects a cluster of naturally affordable walk-ups.

Legal counsel reminded the body that findings of fact in the staff report will be read closely if litigation follows. That warning slowed the rhetoric more effectively than the gavel.

The next hearing will tell whether the revised map is a genuine compromise or a device to run out the calendar. Housing advocates say they will measure acres and months, not adjectives.

Lobbyists flooded the anteroom with one-pagers that recycled last year’s arguments in new fonts. Staffers sorted them into three piles: housing supply, neighborhood character, and process complaints.

Council member Ruth Adeyemi pushed for a narrower map that protects a cluster of naturally affordable walk-ups. Council member Glen Parry argued for the original boundaries, saying “half measures produce half homes.”

Housing advocates noted that delay itself is a policy. “Every month of process is a month of rents,” said organizer Leah Fonseca outside chambers.

Staff will return with a revised map and a displacement-risk analysis in three weeks. A second hearing is scheduled before any final vote.

In the meantime, two pending projects sit in limbo, their financing clocks louder than the gavel.

Outside chambers, organizers collected emails for a rapid-response list. Inside, the parliamentarian struggled to keep amendments within scope as members tested the edges of the draft.

Regional media coverage split along familiar lines. Morning radio framed the delay as chaos; policy newsletters framed it as normal legislating under pressure.

People closest to the story keep returning to a practical question: what changes on Monday morning. Titles and press notes matter less than schedules, budgets, and who answers the phone.
