The Coastal Alpine Club has moved reservations for its eleven mountain huts online, ending a paper lottery that ran for thirty-eight years and a telephone booking window that opened at 9 a.m. on the first Tuesday of March and, in practice, rewarded whoever could spend a morning redialing.

The portal opened on 1 April with 4,180 bunk-nights available across the season. It sold 61 percent of them in the first eleven days — a pace that startled the club's own board, which had budgeted for a slow first year and a fallback to paper if the system faltered.

Real-time availability is the headline change. The portal shows bunk counts per hut per night, group size caps, and weather-related closures pushed directly from the club's warden network. A staff member still reviews a defined set of edge cases by hand: research parties needing multi-night blocks, accessibility requests that require a ground-floor bunk or a warden's assistance, and itinerary rescrambles when a storm closes a col mid-traverse.

> Some longtime members mourn the ritual of the paper form.

The mourning is real and, the club concedes, not merely sentimental. Under the lottery, a member filled out a card in February listing three preferred weeks, mailed it, and waited. The system was slow, opaque, and — crucially — blind. It did not care how fast your connection was or whether you understood a booking calendar.

“I filled out that card with my father for twenty-two years,” said Tomas Verhaeg, who has been a member since 1988 and served two terms on the huts committee. “I am not claiming it was better. I am claiming that a lottery is honest about being luck, and a portal pretends to be fairness while quietly handing the huts to whoever has fast internet and a free Tuesday morning. Those are not the same people who used to get them.”

Club director Helen Park does not entirely disagree, but she has the cancellation data. Under the paper system, roughly 19 percent of booked bunk-nights went unused each season while waitlists sat at fifty or sixty names, because a member who could not go had no practical way to release a bed. In the portal's first month, released bunks were rebooked at a median of nine hours.

“Nostalgia is not a reservation system,” Park said. “I have enormous affection for the card. I also have eleven wardens who spent every August looking at empty bunks and a stack of letters from people who wanted them.”

The club made two concessions to Verhaeg's objection. A quarter of each hut's inventory is held back and released in a randomized draw seven days out, open to anyone who registers interest — deliberately not first-come. And a telephone booking line remains staffed on Wednesday and Friday afternoons, with 240 bunk-nights per season reserved for it. Uptake so far has been modest: 38 bookings by phone against 2,510 online.

No-show penalties tighten this year. A member who fails to appear without releasing the booking forfeits the fee and loses draw priority for one season; a second offense within three years suspends booking privileges for twelve months. The fees do not go to a dividend — the club is a nonprofit and has said the money is earmarked for hut maintenance, with the current backlog centered on the Ravensfoot roof and the Kessel Pass water catchment.

Winter routes remain a separate matter. Booking a winter hut requires a skill acknowledgment naming the specific route and the party's avalanche training, reviewed by a warden before confirmation. Park was direct about the limits of software here. “The website cannot replace judgment. It can only make sure someone with judgment reads the form.”

The transport link is where itineraries still break. Most huts are reached from the coast by a ferry-to-bus sequence, and a missed connection turns a calm plan into a scramble. The club has begun publishing recommended buffer hours alongside each hut listing and has rewritten its refund rules so that a weather-caused ferry cancellation now triggers an automatic credit rather than an appeal.

Local guides have adapted around the same constraint, packaging half-day walks that end before the last dark ferry slot. Two of the region's guiding outfits say the change has measurably reduced stranded-visitor calls to the volunteer rescue line — from 31 in the previous season to 12.

The broader question the club has not answered is labor. If shoulder-season bookings keep climbing, the huts will need wardens in May and October, which means year-round employment rather than seasonal hires, and a payroll the membership fee does not currently cover.

Verhaeg has booked three nights at Ravensfoot in September. He used the portal. He mentioned this without being asked, in the tone of a man reporting a small defeat, and then noted that the confirmation email arrived in four seconds and that he had, against his own expectations, kept it.
